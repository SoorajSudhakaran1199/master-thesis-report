\section{Fundamentals of Industrial Robotics}
\label{sec:fundamentals-industrial-robotics}
Industrial robots are central components of modern manufacturing systems, as they provide precise execution, reduce effort and processing time, and increase productivity and quality. According to international standards, industrial robots are reprogrammable and capable of performing multiple tasks with three or more programmable axes \cite{ISO8373}.

Such systems are widely used in manufacturing operations where stable and repeatable motion is required to ensure high productivity and precision. A typical industrial robot consists of several actuated joints that provide the \ac{dof} capability required to position and orient the end-effector in three-dimensional space. This task is usually defined by specifying the pose of the \ac{tcp}, which represents the reference point for robot motion execution, while the robot flange serves as the physical interface to which the tool is attached \cite{craig2005robotics}.

Robot programming can be understood across different levels of abstraction. At a lower level, the operator manually defines the program by specifying poses, trajectories, and process sequences. At a higher level, the user specifies only the desired output, while the system automatically derives and executes the required motion commands. In industrial practice, manual programming and offline programming are still widely used \cite{Pan2012Survey}. Research on higher-level and skill-based programming aims to reduce the need for low-level programming effort \cite{Kruger2019}.

This shift is particularly important for applications that require high repeatability, frequent adaptation to different workpieces, and operation in high-production environments. In applications such as robotic sheet metal bending, the robot must handle varying workpiece geometries, limited workspace, tool access constraints, and required reorientation steps. Consequently, the task becomes more complex and demanding than conventional pick-and-place operations, thereby motivating the need for advanced robot programming methods, press brake automation, and integrated planning approaches.

\section{Sheet Metal Bending and Press Brake Systems}
\label{sec:sheet-metal-bending-press-brake-systems}
Sheet metal bending is a simple manufacturing process where a sheet of metal is bent into the required shape through plastic deformation. Sheet metal forming processes are widely used in industrial manufacturing due to their ability to produce a variety of products with different geometrical shapes \cite{KalpakjianManufacturing2014}. In the case of press brake operations, sheet metal is positioned between the forming tools such as the punch and die.

Industrial robots have been used in modern manufacturing to perform activities such as handling materials, processing, and assembling parts without relying heavily on humans.\cite{Groover2016}
Figure~\ref{fig:press-brake-system} illustrates a typical robotic press brake setup, where an industrial robot is integrated with the bending machine to automate handling operations.

In automated press brake cells, the robot typically picks the flat sheet, positions it at the tooling, supports it during bending, reorients it between process steps, and unloads the final component. This automation can improve repeatability and reduce direct operator involvement, particularly in repetitive production settings \cite{Aomura2002RoboticBending}. However, the process is difficult to fully automate because each bend alters the physical shape of the part, requiring regripping and repositioning, which in turn necessitate updated grip planning for subsequent process steps.

\subsection{Process Characteristics and Automation Challenges}
\label{subsec:process-characteristics-challenges}

The changing geometry throughout the bending process is one of the defining characteristics of sheet metal bending. A motion that is feasible for one bend may become infeasible after the completion of a previous bending step, as the part envelope, tool accessibility, and boundary conditions change. Therefore, process planning must consider the bend sequence, tooling capability, and handling strategy jointly rather than as isolated decisions. These parameters must be continuously adapted in response to geometric changes during the process \cite{Gupta1998SheetMetalBending}.

For robotic handling, these process characteristics introduce several practical challenges. The robot must maintain a stable grasp of the partially formed workpiece, operate within its reachable workspace, and preserve an appropriate orientation for subsequent operations. In many cases, a single grasp is not sufficient to complete the full bending sequence due to varying bending sides and reachability limitations. To address this, the workpiece must be temporarily placed down and regrasped in a different configuration. This requires the generation of additional trajectories and coordinated motion plans to ensure that the process can be completed without violating kinematic and reachability constraints.

These issues are introduced here as process-level constraints; the detailed planning problems of regripping, grasp selection, and trajectory generation are discussed later in Section~\ref{sec:robotic-sheet-metal-bending-system}.

\section{Manual and Teach Pendant Programming}
\label{sec:manual-teach-pendant-programming}

There are several methods for programming industrial robots in manufacturing environments. One common approach is manual programming using a teach pendant. In this approach, the operator defines the robot's desired positions by entering Cartesian or joint coordinates. These positions are stored as waypoints and executed in a predefined sequence, which must be arranged manually using a robot controller or through a virtual simulation environment \cite{Siciliano2016Robotics}.

For this purpose, a robotic controller and \ac{hmi} devices, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:teach-pendant}, or a virtual simulation environment are required. Teach pendant programming remains useful because it provides the operator with direct control over the robot and allows real-time verification through Cartesian movements or joint-based inputs via \acs{hmi} devices. For stable, repetitive, and well-defined tasks, this method is practical and reliable.

However, when the process involves a large number of motion coordinates or requires frequent adaptation to new workpiece geometries and changing workpiece placements, significant limitations become apparent.

\subsection{Limitations of Manual Teach Pendant Programming}
\label{subsec:limitations-manual-teach-pendant-programming}

The main limitation of manual teach pendant programming is the high level of expert effort required. The operator must have a thorough understanding of robot kinematics, safety guidelines, coordinate systems, robot specifications, tool frames, reachability, and motion constraints.

As task complexity increases, the number of required waypoints also increases. To achieve smooth motion, many intermediate waypoints must be defined, which significantly increases programming effort. Consequently, the programming process becomes slower, highly dependent on individual expertise, and more time-consuming for program generation.

Manual programming also requires frequent reprogramming when the geometry of the workpiece changes. Even if the overall process logic remains similar, the robot program often needs to be adapted, which further increases effort. This makes manual programming inefficient for flexible, high-productivity environments with high part variation \cite{Pan2012Survey}. In addition, constraints related to reachability, safety, and path feasibility must be continuously verified, further increasing the complexity of the programming process.


\subsection{Transition Towards Automated Programming}
\label{subsec:transition-towards-automated-programming}

Robotic sheet metal bending clearly highlights these limitations. The constraints of manual programming have motivated the development of approaches that increase the level of abstraction. Instead of manually defining every pose, engineers increasingly employ virtual commissioning, offline programming, task-level programming, and rule-based planning to reduce direct interaction with the robot.

These approaches do not eliminate engineering effort entirely; however, they shift a significant portion of the workload from manual waypoint teaching to model-based planning and validation, thereby improving consistency, repeatability, and overall efficiency. These challenges have further led to the adoption of advanced programming strategies, particularly virtual commissioning and offline programming, which shift programming effort from the shop floor to a simulation-driven environment. The following section discusses these approaches in detail.


\section{Offline Programming Approaches for Industrial Robots}
\label{sec:offline-programming-approaches}

\ac{olp} approaches enable robot programs to be prepared and tested in a virtual environment before execution on a physical robot. Engineers use \ac{cad} models and simulation tools to define robot motions and generate executable code. These models allow engineers to define paths, evaluate reachability, identify errors, and ensure compliance with safety guidelines. By shifting programming activities to a virtual simulation environment, setup time is reduced and trial-and-error during production is minimised \cite{Pan2012Survey}.

Recent developments in industrial robotics emphasize flexibility and rapid adaptation to new tasks. Offline programming supports these requirements by enabling efficient reprogramming and validation without interrupting ongoing production processes. In applications such as robotic sheet metal bending, offline programming plays a critical role due to the complexity of the process. Each bending operation changes the geometry of the workpiece, requiring careful re-planning of robot motion. Simulation-based validation in offline programming environments allows engineers to evaluate these constraints prior to deployment, thereby improving reliability and process efficiency.

Despite these advantages, offline programming does not fully resolve the programming challenges. The quality of the generated program depends on the accuracy of the virtual model and its calibration with the physical system. Deviations between the virtual and real environments may require additional adjustments during commissioning. Furthermore, many offline programming tools still require the engineer to manually define task sequences, waypoints, and process logic. Although the virtual environment can validate programs without a physical robot, it does not inherently determine optimal grasp positions or automatically adapt to frequent changes, often requiring reprogramming.

These limitations reduce the effectiveness of conventional offline programming for processes such as robotic sheet metal bending, where task geometry and constraints evolve continuously during execution.

\subsection{Digital Twin and Simulation-Based Programming}
\label{subsec:digital-twin-simulation-programming}

The digital twin concept extends offline programming by linking a virtual representation with the physical robot system. A digital twin supports monitoring, validation, and adjustment by combining model-based simulation with data from the real process. In manufacturing, digital twin approaches are considered part of smart production systems, as they support analysis, synchronization, and cyber-physical integration \cite{Tao2021DigitalTwin}.

In industrial robotics, tools such as RoboDK, ABB RobotStudio, KUKA.Sim, and drag\&bot provide virtual environments for programming and validation. These software platforms include robot models that represent kinematic behaviour and enable the detection of errors within the simulation environment. Such capabilities make simulation-based programming particularly valuable for complex robotic cells, where direct trial-and-error programming would be inefficient or pose operational risks.

However, manual intervention is still required, particularly in defining programming and sequencing operations. These approaches do not fully eliminate programming challenges, as waypoints and programs must still be manually defined and implemented. In addition, tasks such as regripping and reorientation often require manual reprogramming, which highlights the limitations of these methods in achieving fully automated and adaptable robot programming.


\section{Automatic Robot Program Generation and Rule-Based Approaches}
\label{sec:automatic-robot-program-generation-rule-based}

Automatic robot program generation aims to reduce the need for manual programming by deriving robot actions from task descriptions, process parameters, geometric inputs, or predefined rules. Instead of explicitly programming every motion, as in manual teach pendant or conventional offline programming methods, the system computes feasible actions based on available information and executes them according to defined rules and constraints.

\subsection{Task-Level Programming and Abstraction}
\label{subsec:task-level-programming-abstraction}

Task-level programming is a key approach used in rule-based systems to automate robot programming. In this approach, the user specifies the desired task outcome, while the system determines how the task should be executed. Early work introduced abstraction layers and symbolic task descriptions to separate task intent from low-level robot motion \cite{LozanoPerez1983}.

Recent approaches utilise skills, motion primitives, and reusable task modules. Operations such as grasping, placing, or constrained motion are represented as modular skills, which can be combined to execute complex robotic programs \cite{Kruger2019}. While this improves flexibility and reduces low-level programming effort, the system still requires mechanisms to select valid actions based on workpiece constraints and to verify whether the resulting motions are feasible and executable.

\subsection{Rule-Based and Constraint-Based Planning}
\label{subsec:rule-based-constraint-based-planning}

Rule-based planning is commonly applied in industrial robotics by executing robot programs based on predefined rules. These rules define how a workpiece should be handled during the process, including allowable orientations, grasp configurations, and motion types. Constraint-based planning extends this concept by explicitly evaluating geometric, kinematic, and process-related conditions before selecting a solution \cite{Pan2012Survey}.

Such methods are widely used because they provide deterministic behaviour in well-defined applications. However, as product variability increases in terms of geometry, positioning, and process constraints, the engineering effort required to define, adapt, and validate these rules also increases. In robotic sheet metal bending, this issue is particularly significant because bend sequencing, tooling, grasp selection, and robot motion planning are closely coupled \cite{Pan2012Survey,Gupta1998SheetMetalBending}.

For complex tasks, the main challenge is not only decision-making but also the reliable coordination of multiple planning components within the process. Grasp planning, regrip planning, trajectory generation, and reachability checking are interdependent and influence each other. If these components are addressed independently, the resulting overall process may become infeasible. Therefore, research in robotic manipulation emphasizes the importance of integrated planning frameworks, particularly in scenarios where grasping and motion constraints are tightly coupled \cite{bohg2014data}.

\section{Robotic Sheet Metal Bending Systems}
\label{sec:robotic-sheet-metal-bending-system}

Robotic sheet metal bending systems combine an industrial manipulator with a press brake to automate the handling and positioning of sheet metal parts. The robot picks the sheet, aligns it with the tooling, supports it during the bending process, and prepares subsequent process steps. This integration reduces direct human handling and effort while enabling precise product output; however, it also introduces planning challenges that are more complex than conventional material transfer tasks.

The robot typically picks the sheet placed in front of the press brake, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:press-brake-system}, and supports the workpiece during bending, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:robotic-bending-system}. This setup represents a typical robotic bending system, where the robot operates in coordination with the press brake to execute a sequence of manipulation and forming operations.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/robotic_bending_system.png}
\caption[Robotic sheet metal bending system with integrated robot and press brake]{Robotic sheet metal bending system with integrated robot and press brake \cite{Neto2013OLP}.}
\label{fig:robotic-bending-system}

The process typically starts with a flat sheet, and after each bending operation, the geometry of the workpiece changes. This affects grasp accessibility, tool clearance, and reachable robot configurations, and some grasp positions may become unreachable due to kinematic constraints. Research in robotic bending has shown that successful automation depends on coordinating process planning with robot motion planning \cite{Gupta1998SheetMetalBending}. The following subsections discuss the main planning aspects relevant to this thesis.

\subsection{Regrip Planning in Sheet Metal Bending}
\label{subsec:regrip-planning-sheet-metal-bending}

Regripping is often necessary when the robot cannot execute all bending steps using a single grasp. The required orientation for subsequent bends may conflict with the initial grasp, or the formed geometry may make the original grasp unsuitable. In such cases, the robot places the workpiece at an intermediate location and performs a regrip in a new configuration.

From a robotic perspective, a valid regrip sequence must consider grasp stability, reachability, and the availability of intermediate placements \cite{wan2017regrasp}. However, in rule-based approaches, regrip planning is often executed through predefined sequences or manually designed rules, which are reliable for known parts and geometries but reduce flexibility when the workpiece geometry or bend sequence changes.

The challenge is that regrip planning is not limited to selecting a new grasp. A regrip decision directly affects subsequent trajectory generation and the accessibility of future bending steps. Therefore, regrip planning cannot be treated independently from motion planning. In robotic press brake bending, tooling, grasping, and motion planning are tightly coupled, and a valid bending strategy must be evaluated across the entire process sequence rather than at isolated steps \cite{Gupta1998SheetMetalBending}.

In existing workflows, grasp selection, regrip decisions, and trajectory validation are often performed as separate steps. This separation can lead to inconsistencies, such as a grasp that is stable but unreachable, a regrip pose that is geometrically valid but difficult to approach, or a trajectory that becomes infeasible after subsequent bending operations. These dependencies highlight the importance of integrated planning for scalable and consistent robotic bending.

\subsection{Grasp Planning for Sheet Metal Handling}
\label{subsec:grasp-planning-sheet-metal-handling}

Grasp planning for sheet metal handling is particularly challenging because sheet metal parts are thin, often large relative to their thickness, and may deform or slip if the grasp is not properly selected. Vacuum grippers are commonly used to handle flat sheet surfaces without requiring mechanical clamping around the part. Their performance depends on suction cup placement, contact quality, available holding force, and load distribution during motion \cite{VacuumGripperModeling}.

General grasp planning methods consider object geometry, contact conditions, and task constraints when selecting stable grasp configurations \cite{bohg2014data}. In sheet metal bending, the selected grasp must also ensure that the bend line and tool access regions remain unobstructed. A grasp that is suitable for handling the initial flat sheet may interfere with subsequent bending operations, particularly after the workpiece geometry changes. Therefore, grasp planning must be integrated with the process sequence rather than being based solely on the initial geometry.

\subsection{Trajectory Planning in Robotic Bending Systems}
\label{subsec:trajectory-planning-robotic-bending-systems}

Trajectory planning in robotic bending systems must generate motions that are collision-free, reachable, and compatible with process constraints. The robot operates in close proximity to the punch, die, workpiece, and other cell components, leaving limited space for avoidance maneuvers. At the same time, it must maintain appropriate orientation to ensure accurate positioning of the sheet during bending.

Classical trajectory generation methods provide a foundation for smooth and continuous robot motion \cite{Gasparetto2012Trajectory}. However, robotic bending introduces additional complexity due to continuous changes in workpiece geometry. The planner must account for evolving collision conditions, workspace limitations, potential singularities, and coordination with grasp and regrip decisions \cite{Gupta1998SheetMetalBending}.

In many industrial systems, these challenges are addressed using conservative motion templates or manually defined waypoints. While this improves reliability, it limits adaptability and flexibility when process conditions change.

\subsection{Limitations of Existing Robotic Bending Systems}
\label{subsec:limitations-existing-robotic-bending-systems}

Existing robotic bending systems demonstrate that press brake automation is technically feasible; however, several limitations remain. Many systems rely on predefined handling strategies and carefully engineered programs. While effective for stable production scenarios, they are less suitable for flexible environments where part geometry and process requirements frequently change.

Although rule-based approaches reduce effort compared to manual and offline programming, a key limitation is the separation of planning tasks. Grasp planning, regrip planning, trajectory generation, and process validation are often treated independently. This separation can result in fragmented workflows, where decisions that are valid in one stage lead to infeasibility in another. For example, a selected grasp may be stable but unsuitable for subsequent motion, or a predefined trajectory may fail when the workpiece geometry changes.

Another limitation is the continued reliance on expert intervention. Even with simulation and offline programming tools, engineers are often required to define motion sequences, verify reachability, adjust waypoints, and validate process logic manually. This increases setup time and reduces adaptability to new parts.

These limitations highlight the need for a unified approach that integrates constraint-aware planning, automated trajectory generation, and structured execution to ensure consistent, repeatable, and flexible robotic bending operations.

\section{Industrial Software Platforms and System Implementations}
\label{sec:industrial-software-platforms-system-implementations}

Industrial robot applications are commonly developed using software platforms that support programming, simulation, validation, and execution. These platforms play a central role in bridging theoretical methods and practical deployment by providing structured tools for developing, testing, and deploying robotic workflows in industrial environments.

\subsection{Simulation and Offline Programming Environments}
\label{subsec:simulation-offline-programming-environments}

Simulation and offline programming environments enable robot programs to be developed, tested, and validated in a virtual environment before execution on a physical system. These environments typically use \acs{cad}-based models of the robot, tooling, and workcell, allowing engineers to evaluate reachability, analyse motion, and visualise robot behaviour prior to deployment. Such capabilities have been widely reported in the literature as key advantages of offline programming, particularly in reducing commissioning time and minimising trial-and-error on the shop floor \cite{Pan2012Survey}.

In addition, modern industrial tools such as RoboDK provide functionalities for motion simulation and integration with multiple robot controllers, further supporting practical deployment in manufacturing environments \cite{RoboDK2024}. However, despite these advantages, offline programming environments are not fully automated planning systems. These systems rely on manual configuration within the software, where the user is responsible for defining task sequences, selecting key poses, and configuring the logical structure of the process. For complex manipulation tasks, this manual configuration remains a significant part of the engineering effort, limiting scalability in flexible production scenarios.

\subsection{Graphical Programming and Integrated Execution Systems}
\label{subsec:graphical-programming-integrated-execution-systems}

Graphical programming platforms, such as drag\&bot, aim to reduce the complexity of robot programming by allowing users to construct workflows using modular function blocks \cite{dragandbot_about_2026}. The AnyRobot interface enables external software to communicate with the drag\&bot runtime through a WebSocket-based interface \cite{dragandbot_anyrobot_2025}. Together, the graphical workflow environment and the runtime interface support the development of robotic applications without requiring extensive low-level programming knowledge.

In the context of this thesis, drag\&bot is relevant because it provides an integrated drag-and-drop programming and execution environment \cite{dragandbot_about_2026}. This environment can be combined with the AnyRobot interface for runtime-connected robotic workflow deployment \cite{dragandbot_anyrobot_2025}. The KEBA/drag\&bot relationship also provides the industrial context for selecting the platform in this work \cite{keba_dragandbot_2022}.

These platforms are effective for structured tasks where predefined modules can be reused and combined efficiently. However, similar to offline programming environments, graphical systems generally rely on user-defined workflows and logic. While they facilitate execution and monitoring, they do not inherently solve the planning problem.

In particular, they do not automatically determine key decisions such as grasp selection, regrip strategies, or trajectory generation in the presence of changing workpiece geometries. These decisions remain dependent on user input or external planning methods. This limitation is especially critical in robotic sheet metal bending, where grasping, regripping, and motion planning are strongly interdependent and must be coordinated across the process sequence.

\subsection{Summary and Limitations of Industrial Platforms}
\label{subsec:summary-limitations-industrial-platforms}

Existing industrial software platforms provide powerful tools for simulation, validation, and execution of robotic applications. They significantly improve programming efficiency compared to manual approaches and support practical deployment in industrial environments. However, their primary limitation lies in the lack of integrated automatic planning capabilities. While these platforms assist in implementing and validating robot programs, they still require substantial manual input for task definition, decision-making, and coordination of planning components.

This limitation becomes increasingly significant in complex applications such as robotic sheet metal bending, where multiple interdependent planning tasks must be considered simultaneously. These observations highlight the need for approaches that go beyond tool-based programming and provide integrated, constraint-aware, and automated planning capabilities, which motivates the work presented in this thesis.

\subsection{Critical Comparison of Existing Approaches}
\label{subsec:critical-comparison-existing-approaches}

The reviewed approaches differ not only in their level of automation, but also in the degree to which they integrate planning decisions into an executable industrial workflow. Manual and teach-pendant programming provide direct control but rely heavily on operator expertise. Offline programming and digital-twin approaches improve simulation and validation, but they often still require manual sequence definition, accurate model preparation, and repeated engineering adjustment when workpiece conditions change. Academic planning methods provide strong solutions for individual subproblems such as grasp selection, regripping, or trajectory generation, but they are frequently not connected to the complete runtime workflow of an industrial press-brake cell.

\section{Research Gap and Motivation}
\label{sec:research-gap-motivation}

The literature shows a clear progression from manual robot programming toward simulation-supported and partially automated methods. Manual programming remains reliable for stable tasks; however, it is not well suited to scenarios where workpiece geometry, position, and regrip orientation change frequently. Offline programming improves safety and commissioning efficiency, yet it still depends on user-defined task sequences and accurate virtual models. Automatic programming and rule-based methods further reduce manual effort, but many approaches focus on individual decisions rather than the complete bending workflow.

In robotic sheet metal bending, this separation represents a major limitation. The workpiece changes shape after each bending step, which affects grasp feasibility, regrip requirements, and reachable trajectories. A grasp that is valid at one stage may obstruct tool access in a later step; a regrip pose may be geometrically feasible but difficult to reach; and a trajectory that is valid in isolation may become infeasible once the formed part is attached to the robot.

The identified research gap is the lack of a practical workflow that integrates grasp planning, regrip planning, and constraint-aware trajectory generation for robotic press brake bending. Existing methods reduce manual effort and improve precision compared to manual press brake operation; however, they do not sufficiently address these interdependent planning tasks as a coordinated process under workspace, kinematic, and process constraints.

Addressing this gap is essential for flexible automation. A system that can generate valid trajectories, intermediate configurations, and decision processes such as regrip calculations, and transform these into executable robot motions, can significantly reduce engineering effort while improving adaptability, consistency, and repeatability.

This thesis therefore proposes an integrated and constraint-aware approach in which automated trajectory generation, workspace-aware planning, and deterministic execution are combined within a structured pipeline. The role of the user is limited to specifying the desired output, while the system processes this input and decomposes it into executable tasks.

The identified gap and the proposed direction form the basis for the concept development presented in the following chapter.
